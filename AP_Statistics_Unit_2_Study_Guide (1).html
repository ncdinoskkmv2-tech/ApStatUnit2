Chapter 7

Scatterplots, Association, and Correlation

Goal: describe the relationship between two quantitative variables, and understand what a correlation number can—and cannot—tell you.

1. Identify the two variables

  • Quantitative variable: a numerical measurement for which arithmetic is meaningful, such as height, time, distance, or test score.
  • Explanatory variable: the variable used to explain or predict changes in another variable. It is usually placed on the horizontal axis and called x.
  • Response variable: the outcome being explained or predicted. It is usually placed on the vertical axis and called y.
Choosing x as the explanatory variable does not prove that x causes y. The roles come from the question and study design.

2. Describe a scatterplot using D-F-S-U

Always describe direction, form, strength, and unusual features.

Direction

Positive: larger x-values tend to go with larger y-values.
Negative: larger x-values tend to go with smaller y-values.

Form

Linear: roughly straight.
Nonlinear: curved or another systematic shape.

Strength

How tightly the points follow the overall pattern. Less scatter around a clear pattern means a stronger association.

Unusual features

Look for outliers, clusters, gaps, separate groups, or points far from the main pattern.

Conceptual illustration: describe the pattern you actually see, not just the slope of a line drawn through it.

AP response template
“There is a [weak, moderate, or strong], [positive or negative], [linear or curved] association between [explanatory variable with units] and [response variable with units]. [Describe any unusual points or clusters.]”

3. Correlation: the strength and direction of a linear relationship

The correlation coefficient, pronounced “r,” is a number that summarizes the direction and strength of a linear association between two quantitative variables.

Correlation r = average of the products of the paired standardized values, with the sample adjustment.

In a statistics course, you usually let your calculator compute r. The important part is knowing how to interpret it.

CorrelationMeaning
Close to +1Strong positive linear association.
Close to −1Strong negative linear association.
Close to 0Weak linear association. A curved relationship may still be present.
Exactly +1 or −1All points lie perfectly on a straight line with positive or negative slope.
Do not use a universal cutoff for “strong.” Strength depends on the context and the scatterplot. Always look at the graph.
Interactive example: see positive, negative, and weak linear patterns

Choose a pattern and observe the example points. These are illustrations, not data from your class.

Positive pattern: as x increases, y generally increases.

Conditions for correlation

  • Quantitative variables: both variables are quantitative.
  • Straight Enough: the relationship is reasonably linear.
  • Outlier Condition: investigate unusual points that could distort the correlation.

4. Properties of correlation

  • Between −1 and +1: correlation cannot be outside this range.
  • Sign gives direction: positive or negative.
  • Symmetric: swapping x and y does not change correlation.
  • Unitless: correlation has no units.
  • Shift and positive-scale invariant: adding a constant or converting units with a positive multiplier does not change correlation.
  • Outlier-sensitive: one unusual point can increase, decrease, or reverse correlation.
  • Linear only: correlation does not capture every possible relationship.
If one variable is multiplied by a negative number, its order reverses and the sign of the correlation reverses. Its magnitude stays the same.

5. Association does not prove causation

  • Lurking variable: a variable not included in the analysis that influences the apparent relationship between the variables being studied.
  • Confounding: the effects of variables are entangled, making it difficult to isolate a causal effect.
  • Observational data can show association but generally cannot, on their own, prove that one variable causes another.
  • A properly designed randomized experiment can provide stronger evidence about causation.
Common trap: “The variables are strongly correlated, so x causes y” is not a valid conclusion from correlation alone.

Chapter 8

Linear Regression

Goal: fit a line to a linear relationship, make predictions, and interpret the line and its errors in context.

1. The least-squares regression line

A regression line summarizes the linear relationship between an explanatory variable and a response variable.

Predicted response = intercept + slope × explanatory value
ŷ = b₀ + b₁xRead “ŷ” as “y-hat,” meaning the response value predicted by the line.

Predicted response, ŷ

The value the model predicts for a particular x-value.

Intercept, b₀

The predicted response when x equals zero.

Slope, b₁

The predicted change in the response for a one-unit increase in x.

The least-squares regression line is the line that makes the sum of the squared vertical residuals as small as possible.

Choose the line that minimizes: the sum of (observed response − predicted response)².

Squaring keeps positive and negative errors from cancelling and gives greater weight to larger errors.

2. How the calculator finds the line

For a data set with a linear relationship, the slope and intercept can be calculated from summary statistics:

Slope = correlation × (standard deviation of y ÷ standard deviation of x)
b₁ = r × sᵧ ÷ sₓ
Intercept = mean of y − slope × mean of x
b₀ = ȳ − b₁ x̄

These formulas explain how the regression line is connected to correlation and the means and standard deviations. You can use the calculator to do the arithmetic, but you must still interpret the result.

The regression line passes through the point made by the mean x-value and mean y-value. Its slope has the same sign as the correlation.

3. Interpret the slope and intercept

Slope template
“For each additional [one unit of x], the model predicts that [y] will [increase/decrease] by about [slope] [units of y], on average.”
Intercept template
“When [x equals zero in context], the model predicts [intercept] [units of y]. This is [meaningful/not meaningful] because [explain whether zero is possible and within the data range].”
  • Slope units are response-units per explanatory-unit.
  • Intercept units are the same as the response variable.
  • An intercept may have no practical meaning if x = 0 is impossible or far outside the observed data.
  • A regression equation describes a relationship; it does not automatically describe a causal effect.

4. Residuals: prediction errors

Residual = observed response − predicted response
e = y − ŷ

Positive residual

The actual point lies above the line. The model predicted too low.

Negative residual

The actual point lies below the line. The model predicted too high.

Zero residual

The actual point lies on the regression line.

The vertical gap from the observed point to the fitted line is the residual. A positive residual is above the line; a negative residual is below it.

  • For an ordinary least-squares line with an intercept, the residuals add to approximately zero.
  • The residuals have no linear association with x for that fitted line.

5. The coefficient of determination: R-squared

R-squared describes the percentage of variation in the response variable that is accounted for by its linear regression on the explanatory variable.

For simple linear regression: R-squared = correlation × correlation
R² = r²
Interpretation template
“About [R² written as a percentage] of the variation in [response variable] is accounted for by the linear regression of [response] on [explanatory variable].”
  • R-squared is between 0 and 1, or 0% and 100%.
  • It has no positive or negative direction.
  • It does not mean that the model predicts that percentage of individual observations correctly.
  • A high R-squared does not prove causation or guarantee a suitable model.

6. Standard deviation of the residuals

This describes the typical vertical distance of the observations from the regression line, in the response variable's units.

Residual standard deviation = square root of [sum of squared residuals ÷ (number of observations − 2)]

A smaller residual standard deviation generally means the observed points are closer to the fitted line, when comparing models for the same response and comparable data.

7. What changes when the data are rescaled?

ChangeWhat happens
Change the units of xThe slope changes to match the new x-unit. Equivalent predictions remain the same after unit conversion.
Add a constant to every x-valueCorrelation and slope stay the same; intercept changes.
Add a constant to every y-valueCorrelation and slope stay the same; intercept shifts by that constant.
Multiply y by a constantSlope, intercept, residuals, and residual standard deviation scale with y. R-squared stays the same.
Swap x and yCorrelation stays the same, but the regression line generally changes.

8. Regression to the mean

When the relationship is not perfectly correlated, predictions for unusually high or low explanatory values tend to be closer to the response mean than the corresponding extremes. This is a statistical tendency, not a guarantee that any individual will change or improve.

Chapter 9

Regression Wisdom

Goal: check whether the model is appropriate, identify unusual points, and avoid misleading predictions or causal claims.

1. Residual plots: what the pattern tells you

A residual plot displays residuals against the explanatory variable or the predicted values. A horizontal reference at zero helps you see whether the model systematically misses in one direction.

Random scatter around zero

Supports the use of a straight-line model, with roughly consistent spread.

Curve or wave

Suggests the relationship is not adequately linear.

Fan or funnel

Suggests the residual spread changes as x or the predicted response changes.

Clusters

May indicate subgroups, omitted variables, or a data-collection structure that needs investigation.

Illustrative patterns. Residual plots should be judged for systematic shape and changing spread.

2. Conditions and assumptions

  • Quantitative variables: use quantitative measurements for correlation and regression.
  • Straight Enough: the underlying relationship should be approximately linear.
  • Outlier Condition: investigate points with large residuals or high leverage.
  • Independence: observations should be independent when the study design and inference require it. Repeated measurements and time series may violate this.
  • Equal variance: residuals should have roughly constant vertical spread across x-values or fitted values.
  • Nearly Normal errors: relevant for regression inference; it is not required merely to calculate the regression line.

Your class module especially emphasizes linearity, re-expression, and the importance of residual plots. More assumptions are needed for inference than for fitting a line alone.

3. Outliers, leverage, and influential points

ConceptDefinitionHow to detect it
Outlier / large residualThe observed response is far from the model's predicted response.Inspect the residual plot and original scatterplot.
High leverageThe x-value is far from the mean x-value.Look for extreme explanatory values in the original scatterplot.
Influential pointRemoving the point substantially changes the regression model.Compare the fitted model with and without the point.
  • High leverage does not necessarily mean a large residual.
  • A high-leverage point can pull the fitted line toward itself and change its slope or intercept.
  • Influential points can have small residuals because the line has been pulled toward them.
  • Do not delete an unusual point automatically. Check whether it is an error or a genuine observation, and explain the effect of including it.

4. Extrapolation

Extrapolation means using the fitted line to predict for an x-value outside the range of x-values used to fit the model.

  • It is risky because the observed trend may not continue beyond the data range.
  • Predictions far outside the data range are particularly unreliable.
  • Predictions into the future assume the historical relationship continues at the same rate.
  • Interpolation means predicting within the observed x-range; it is generally less risky, but still depends on the model being suitable.

5. Lurking variables, causation, and groups

  • A regression model does not prove that x causes y. A lurking variable may influence both.
  • Observational data do not rule out every alternative causal explanation.
  • Data should represent a reasonably coherent group. Different subgroups can follow different patterns.
  • Combining groups may hide important relationships or create a misleading overall trend.
  • Data values that are themselves summaries, such as group means, can make relationships appear stronger than those among individuals.

6. Responsible regression report

  1. Identify the explanatory and response variables and their units.
  2. Describe the scatterplot and whether a linear model is reasonable.
  3. Give and interpret the regression equation and relevant statistics.
  4. Inspect residuals for curvature and changing spread.
  5. Discuss unusual points, leverage, influence, and possible subgroups.
  6. Warn about extrapolation and plausible lurking variables.

7. TI-Nspire CX II: residual diagnostics

  1. Calculate the regression using your named x-list and y-list.
  2. If the command allows it, save predicted values and residuals. Otherwise create a predicted-value column from the equation and calculate each residual as observed y minus predicted y.
  3. On a Data & Statistics page, select residuals for the vertical axis and x or predicted values for the horizontal axis.
  4. Look for random scatter around zero and roughly equal spread. A curve, fan, or cluster needs investigation.
  5. To assess influence, compare the regression with and without the unusual point.
A residual plot is useful for checking fit, but high-leverage points can be hard to spot in residual plots alone. Also inspect the original scatterplot and x-values.
Chapter 10

Re-expressing Data: Get It Straight!

Goal: transform variables when a different scale can make the data more linear, more symmetric, or more consistent in spread.

1. What re-expression means

Re-expression means applying a mathematical operation to every value of a variable. It may help make a distribution more symmetric, make spreads more alike, straighten a scatterplot, or make the scatter around a line more consistent.

Re-expression does not guarantee a perfect model. The goal is a useful model that represents the data better.

2. The Ladder of Powers

A useful order of common transformations is shown below. Moving right generally compresses large positive values more strongly.

TransformationWhat it doesPotential use
Square the variableEmphasizes large values relative to small ones.May help certain downward-curving relationships.
Keep original valuesNo change.Use when the relationship is already suitably linear.
Square rootCompresses larger values.Often worth trying for counts or mild right-skew/curvature.
LogarithmCompresses large values more strongly.Often useful for exponential growth or multiplicative patterns.
ReciprocalStrongly compresses large positive values and reverses their order.May help certain curved patterns; requires nonzero values.

This is a guide, not a rigid rule. A negative power reverses the order of positive values. The best transformation depends on the actual scatterplot and residual plot.

3. Choosing a transformation

Upward curve getting steeper

Consider whether exponential growth is plausible. A logarithm of the response may straighten the pattern.

Right-skewed positive values

Try a square root or logarithm and inspect the distribution.

Already straight with stable spread

Keep the original scale; transformation may not help.

Several groups or an irregular pattern

A simple transformation may not fix the problem. Investigate subgroups or another model.

Transform one variable at a time when exploring options. Replot the relationship and check residuals after each transformation.

4. Logarithms turn some curves into lines

Exponential model

Original relationship: y = a × b raised to the power x.
After taking logarithms: log y = log a + x log b.

Plotting the logarithm of y against x can make an exponential relationship linear.

Power model

Original relationship: y = a × x raised to the power b.
After taking logarithms: log y = log a + b log x.

Plotting log y against log x can make a power relationship linear.

  • Base-10 logarithms and natural logarithms often have the same straightening effect, although the coefficients are different.
  • Logarithms require positive values. Zero and negative values cannot be logged directly.
  • Reciprocals require nonzero values. Adding a constant to handle zeros changes the interpretation.

5. Return predictions to the original units

What was transformed?How to return a prediction to the original scale
Square root of ySquare the predicted square-root value.
Base-10 logarithm of yRaise 10 to the predicted log value.
Natural logarithm of yRaise e to the predicted natural-log value.
Reciprocal of yTake the reciprocal of the predicted transformed value, if it is nonzero.
Back-transforming returns the prediction to the original scale. It does not automatically guarantee that the result equals the mean of the original response values.

6. Calculator and technology guidance

  1. In Lists & Spreadsheet, create a new list for the transformed variable so the original data remain intact.
  2. For a square root, calculate the square root of each original value; for a logarithm, use the log or natural-log function; for a reciprocal, calculate one divided by each value.
  3. Use the transformed list in a new scatterplot and fit a regression line.
  4. Inspect the new scatterplot and residual plot. Keep the transformation only if it improves the structure in a meaningful way.
  5. Write down exactly which variable was transformed, then reverse the transformation when interpreting predictions.

The exact entry syntax can depend on how your lists are named. Use the calculator's function catalogue and make sure the new list has the same number of rows as the original.

AP interpretation checklist

0 skills checked

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Using correlation to describe a curved relationship as if it were linear.
  • Forgetting units or context when interpreting a slope.
  • Interpreting the intercept when x = 0 is outside the meaningful range without noting the limitation.
  • Reversing the residual subtraction.
  • Writing “R-squared is the percent of points predicted correctly.”
  • Assuming a small residual proves a point is not influential.
  • Predicting far beyond the observed x-range without warning about extrapolation.
  • Claiming causation from an observational association.
  • Transforming data without checking whether the plots actually improve.
